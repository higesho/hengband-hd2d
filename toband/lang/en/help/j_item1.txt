=== Weapon Guide ===

  Here I explain Western weapons that are not very familiar to Japanese
  readers. Angband includes weapons that are hard to picture even for people
  who are used to RPGs, let alone for those who are not. In addition, some
  weapons seem to be misunderstood because of mistaken RPG reference materials
  and the influence of fantasy novels, so I decided to include an explanation
  of every real weapon that appears in Angband.

  The explanations below use the following format. First, the name of the
  weapon. (In the Japanese edition of Angband, the translated weapon names are
  written in katakana.) Next come specifications such as overall length and
  weight. These were not measured by the author; they are quoted from
  reference works. Then follow the weapon's distinguishing features, how it
  was used, its history, and so on.

  Please note that this explanation emphasizes each item's role as a weapon in
  the real world, rather than its use in the dungeon as in Angband. It is
  therefore not an explanation of how to use the weapons in Angband.

  I am also embarrassed to say that I lacked source material on some weapons,
  so a very few entries may be unreliable. If you find a mistake, I would
  appreciate it if you would let me know. If anything is hard to read or hard
  to understand, I will improve it, so please let me know about that too.

  Finally, I list my references. They are all available at large bookstores,
  so if Western weapons interest you, please have a look.

Daggers

 Dagger
  A dagger in the broad sense, that is, a short blade. Overall length 20-60
  cm, blade width 1-3 cm, weight 0.25-0.5 kg. Many kinds of daggers were used
  from the 12th to the 19th century. The characteristics differed greatly by
  kind, and there were daggers for cutting as well as daggers for stabbing. In
  general, a dagger was less a weapon than a tool of everyday life, and it was
  used as a weapon only for self-defense or in duels. The most common dagger
  is a stabbing dagger 30 cm long. A dagger can also be thrown, but it is
  large and poorly balanced for the purpose, so its range is very short and
  considerable skill is needed to hit with it.

 Main Gauche (also: parrying dagger)
  Overall length 30-50 cm, blade width 1-3 cm, weight 0.3-0.5 kg. A defensive
  dagger used in private duels from the end of the 15th century to the 19th
  century. It was normally used in the swordsmanship called fencing, held in
  the left hand; the name main gauche means "left hand" and comes from this.
  In the right hand a rapier or a small sword was used. Its distinguishing
  features are a stiff blade and either a long, straight crossguard or a guard
  that curves sharply from the hilt toward the blade. The guard and the blade
  were used to deflect the opponent's attacks and to entangle the opponent's
  sword. Of course, the opponent's sword was a rapier or a small sword, so it
  was practically impossible to catch any heavier weapon with it. Outside
  France this dagger was called a parrying dagger, and it came in many forms:
  ones with three blades, ones with a comb-shaped blade (especially called a
  sword breaker), ones with a cup-shaped guard, and so on.


Swords

 Short Sword
  A "short blade". Overall length 70-80 cm, blade width 2-5 cm, weight 0.8-1.8
  kg. A sword used by heavy infantry from the 14th to the 16th century. Its
  features are a broad blade, a sharply pointed tip, and, as the name says,
  shortness (under 80 cm). This is because the short sword was used in close
  combat between heavily armored infantry, and three points were valued: it
  was sturdy; it could thrust, which inflicts a large wound on an armored
  opponent better than hacking does; and it was short enough not to cut one's
  own comrades when swung in a dense crowd. It was used in one hand.

 Long Sword
  A "long blade". Overall length 80-95 cm, blade width 3-5 cm in early
  examples and 2-3 cm in later ones, weight 1.5-2 kg. A sword used on
  horseback by knights and cavalry from the 11th to the 16th century. Its
  features are a straight blade, a sharp tip, and two edges. It was used in
  one hand. Early examples could not technically be made with a thin blade, so
  they were used for hacking; later examples could be made thin enough and
  given a sharp tip, so they could both cut and thrust.

 Broad Sword (also: heavy military sword, schiavona, reiterpallasch)
  A "wide blade". Overall length 70-80 cm, blade width 3-4 cm, weight 1.4-1.6
  kg. A heavy military sword used by cavalry and infantry in the 17th and 18th
  centuries. Its features are a straight blade and two edges, and it was
  intended for hacking. Another feature is a hilt with various designs to
  protect the fist. It does not have a particularly wide blade compared with
  the swords of the Middle Ages, but compared with the rapier, the mainstream
  sword of its day, its blade can be called quite wide, and so it was named
  the broad sword. It was used in one hand. Cavalry did not use it in cavalry
  charges, as they did the sabre; it was used in confused melees of cavalry
  and infantry.

 Bastard Sword (also: hand-and-a-half sword)
  Overall length 115-140 cm, blade width 2-3 cm, weight 2.5-3 kg. A large
  sword used by knights from the 13th to the 17th century, intended for both
  hacking and thrusting. Because it could be used in one hand or in two, it
  was called a bastard sword, "a sword resembling both a one-handed and a
  two-handed sword". Knights fought with this sword and a shield and, when it
  came to it, threw the shield away and struck a decisive blow with the
  bastard sword in both hands. It differs from a two-handed sword in that its
  blade is not so long, so it keeps more mobility, and it differs from a
  one-handed sword in that its hilt is very long (30-40 cm) because it is held
  with both hands. Knights wore this sword at the waist.

 Two-Handed Sword (also: zweihander)
  A "two-handed sword". Overall length 180-200 cm, blade width 4-8 cm, weight
  2.9-6.5 kg. A large sword used by infantry from the 13th to the 16th
  century. It was intended to hack using the weight of the blade, but it has a
  point and can also thrust. Its features are its length and weight, a
  straight blade, and a very long hilt (30-50 cm) because it is held with both
  hands. It was so long and heavy that considerable skill was needed to handle
  it. The soldiers who used this sword could not wear it at the waist because
  of its length, and either carried it on their backs or simply carried it in
  their hands.

 Executioner's Sword (not in TOband)
  A "sword of the executioner". Overall length 100 cm and up, blade width 6-7
  cm, weight 2.2-2.5 kg. A two-handed sword for executions, used by
  executioners in the 17th and 18th centuries. Its features follow from its
  purpose. The sword is meant to sever a head with a single stroke. For this
  reason the blade is very wide, there is no point, and the end is rounded.
  Also, its hilt is much shorter than that of a two-handed sword, so that it
  is easy to bring down with force. It has two edges, and the blade is often
  decorated with various engravings.

 Sabre (also: saber, schweizersabel) (not in TOband)
  A "cavalry sword". Overall length 70-120 cm, blade width 2-4 cm, weight
  1.7-2.4 kg. A sword used as the main sword of cavalry units from the 16th to
  the 20th century. Its features differ considerably with period and region,
  so they cannot be stated in general. Roughly speaking, it is a one-handed,
  single-edged sword with a flexible, curved blade. There are, however, three
  kinds of blade shape: straight, semi-curved, and curved. There are also
  three kinds of tip: spear-shaped, false-edge-shaped (see Cutlass), and
  hatchet-shaped. Among these, the straight blade with a spear-shaped tip was
  made for thrusting, the curved blade with a hatchet-shaped tip for cutting,
  and the semi-curved blade with a false-edge tip for both. The most common
  sabres were those with a semi-curved blade and a false-edge or
  hatchet-shaped tip. The guard and hilt also have regional characteristics
  and developed in various ways. Sabres were given various names by region and
  are sometimes treated as separate weapons. Cavalry who used the sabre used
  it in cavalry charges.

 Rapier (also: epee rapiere, dress sword, espada ropera)
  Overall length 80-100 cm, blade width 1-3 cm, weight 0.7-0.9 kg. A
  one-handed sword used from the 16th to the 18th century. Its features are a
  straight blade, a sharp point, two edges, and a very thin blade. It can cut,
  but it is normally used by thrusting. It was used in fencing, in an age when
  soldiers were not heavily equipped but very lightly clad and carried no
  shields. In the early period a shield was sometimes held in the other hand,
  but in the 16th century the usual stance was a rapier in the right hand and
  a main gauche in the left. It spread widely as the instrument of knights'
  duels and, after those died out, of the private duels of nobles and
  gentlemen.

 Cutlass (not in TOband)
  Overall length 50-60 cm, blade width 3-5 cm, weight 1.2-1.4 kg. A sword used
  by sailors in the 18th and 19th centuries. Its feature is a tip called a
  false edge. A false edge means a blade that is basically single-edged but is
  double-edged from the tip back to about a third of the blade. The blade is
  also very wide and slightly curved. Some had a large guard to protect the
  fist, and some had a saw-toothed back (the side without an edge). It was
  intended for hacking, but the false edge also made it possible to inflict
  large wounds by thrusting. It was used in one hand.

 Small Sword (also: light sword, town sword, walking sword) (not in TOband)
  A "little sword". Overall length 50-70 cm, blade length 40-60 cm, blade
  width 1-1.5 cm, weight 0.51-0.7 kg. A sword that ordinary citizens,
  gentlemen, and nobles carried daily from the 16th to the 18th century. Its
  features are a sharply pointed tip and a thin, straight blade. It was more
  an everyday article than a weapon, and many were made for ceremonies or
  decoration. In private duels and the like it was used in one hand, to
  thrust. It can be called a small rapier; like the rapier it had hilts and
  guards of many shapes, and like the rapier it was used in fencing. As with
  the rapier, a main gauche was sometimes held in the left hand. Those used by
  nobles in particular were very expensive, with showily decorated hilts set
  with jewels.

 Scimitar (also: shamshir, simiterra, cimeterre) (not in TOband)
  A "crescent sword". Overall length 80-100 cm, blade length 75-90 cm, blade
  width 2-3 cm, weight 1.5-2 kg. The typical sword of Persia. Its features are
  a gently curved blade, a hilt that curves in the direction opposite to the
  blade, and a single edge. It is handled in one hand and made to hack by
  being swung down. The pommel of the hilt, called the "lion's head", is also
  a characteristic of this sword. It is the sword from which the sabre
  originated, and in Persian it was called shamshir, "lion's tail". Some
  scimitars had a flamboyant-style blade (a blade with a wavy shape).

 Tulwar (also: tegha) (not in TOband)
  Overall length 70-100 cm, blade width about 2 cm, weight 1.4-1.8 kg. A kind
  of sabre born in 16th-century India, used by the cavalry of the Mughal
  Empire of India, Turkey, Persia, Mongolia, and elsewhere in the 16th and
  17th centuries. Its features are a single-edged, curved blade, a
  cross-shaped guard, and a knuckle guard. The curve of the blade is extreme
  in early examples, but in later ones it comes close to straight.


  * Notes on how swords are used
  Western swords were made and used under a concept entirely different from
  that of Eastern swords. That is, they were not used to cut but to hack. The
  edge of a Western sword certainly has some cutting ability, but compared
  with a Japanese sword, for example, its sharpness is considerably poorer.
  Western warriors did not simply swing the sword down, as Eastern swordsmen
  do, but tried to hack with the force of their whole body weight. The edge
  exists only to narrow the area over which the force is applied at that
  moment, and so to make the blow it delivers greater.

  Moreover, there is a great difference between Western and Eastern
  swordsmanship: armor and shields. In the West, defense depended almost
  entirely on heavy armor and large shields. The weapon was for attacking the
  opponent; blocking or deflecting the opponent's attacks with the weapon
  began only in the 17th century, after armor and shields had gone out of use.
  That is the swordsmanship called fencing. Before that, it was very rare to
  receive an opponent's attack with a sword.


Axes and Hammers

 Beaked Axe (not in TOband)
  A "bearded axe". Overall length about 20 cm. A small single-edged axe, a
  weapon for sea battles used by the Vikings from the 8th to the 11th century.
  It was intended for hacking and was used in one hand. Its feature is the
  shape of the axe head, which projects squarely on its lower side. This
  projection was for hooking onto the side of a ship. The axe also had a long
  haft, and the haft and the projection were useful when boarding from one
  ship to another.

 Battle Axe
  A "war axe". Overall length 80-120 cm, weight 1-1.6 kg. A weapon intended
  for hacking, widely used from the Neolithic age until the early 19th century
  in Eastern Europe. Apart from the single-edged, hatchet-like blade on a long
  haft, its features, like those of the mace, cannot be stated in general. It
  was an infantry weapon used in both hands.

 Broad Axe
  A "wide axe". Overall length 1.5-2 m, axe head length 30-45 cm. A two-handed
  single-edged axe used by the Vikings from the 8th to the 11th century. Its
  feature, as the name says, is an extremely heavy and wide axe head, and it
  was intended for hacking. It had the power to cleave in one blow the chain
  mail that was the main armor of that time.

 Great Axe
  A "large axe". Weight about 4 kg. A double-edged axe used in both hands. As
  the name says, its features are an extremely large and heavy axe head and a
  long haft.

 Mace (only the Lead-Filled Mace appears in TOband)
  A "club with a metal head". Overall length 30-100 cm, weight 2-3 kg. A
  striking weapon used from the 30th century BC to the 17th century. There are
  very many kinds of mace and their features cannot be stated in general, but
  it is basically a club with a complicated head on the end of a haft. Typical
  ones include those with a thick, spiked end, those made of identically
  shaped iron plates joined radially, those with a spiked ball attached (see
  Morning Star), and those with an onion-shaped head. From the Middle Ages on
  it was widely used as a weapon to deliver shock to heavily equipped knights.
  Armor that a sword could not cut could not stop the shock of a mace. It was
  used by knights, but later long-hafted maces for infantry were also made.

 Morning Star (also: morgenstern) (not in TOband)
  A "spiked ball hammer". Overall length 50-80 cm, weight 2-2.5 kg. The weapon
  most favored by knights and soldiers from the 13th to the 17th century. A
  kind of mace, it is characterized by a star-ball-shaped head. That is, many
  spikes project radially from a spherical, cylindrical, or elliptical head.
  It was widely used because it was effective against knights and soldiers in
  heavy armor.

 War Hammer (not in TOband)
  A "battle hammer". Overall length 50-200 cm, weight 1.5-3.5 kg. A striking
  weapon used by Scythian cavalry in the 6th century and by infantry from the
  14th to the 16th century. Its feature is the shape of the head: one side has
  a pointed hammerhead and the other a flat, unpointed hammerhead. Many have a
  spear-shaped tip or a sharply pointed butt. Those for infantry had a long
  haft of about 2 m, those for knights' tournaments were about 80 cm, and
  those that knights used on horseback were about 50 cm. Like other striking
  weapons, it was used to break the opponent's armor and to deliver shock
  through the armor. When people think of a hammer, they tend to imagine one
  with a large head for driving stakes, or a larger version of a claw hammer,
  but the war hammer has a head close to a pickaxe, in order to make the point
  of impact small.

 Flail
  Overall length 30-50 cm, haft length 15-30 cm, weight 1-2 kg. A striking
  weapon used against cavalry by horsemen of the 11th and 12th centuries. Its
  feature is that a short bar (the part that threshes the grain) and a long
  bar (the haft) are joined by a chain. You hold the haft and swing it around,
  giving speed to the head and increasing the force of the blow. The head was
  often made of metal, or, if wooden, fitted with radiating spikes or
  reinforced with metal, with various devices to increase its power.

 Ball-and-Chain (not in TOband)
  Overall length 50-80 cm, weight 1-1.5 kg. A kind of flail in which the head
  is a metal star ball, fixed to the haft by a longer chain than that of the
  flail. It is sometimes confused with the morning star and called by that
  name. How it is used is almost the same as the flail.

 Two-Handed Flail (not in TOband)
  Overall length 160-200 cm, haft length 120-150 cm, weight 2.5-3.5 kg. A
  striking weapon used against cavalry by infantry from the 13th to the early
  20th century. The difference from the flail is the length of the haft. It is
  very long because it is used in both hands, and it produces greater force.
  Because even weak infantry could deal a sufficient blow to heavily equipped
  knights with it, it was widely used in the 14th century.

 Quarterstaff
  A "six-shaku pole". Overall length 180 cm, weight about 1.4 kg. A striking
  weapon used by English farmers. It is only a wooden pole, but its one
  distinguishing feature is that both ends are reinforced with metal. It is
  normally used not by one end only but by both ends. A skilled quarterstaff
  fighter could attack freely by making good use of both ends. However, the
  weakness of this weapon is that it is made of wood: it has no effect at all
  against an opponent in armor, and it is easily broken by iron weapons.

 Whip
  A "lash". Overall length 80-100 cm, weight 0.5-1 kg. A cord-like weapon made
  of leather. By holding the slightly stiffened handle and swinging it, you
  give striking force to the tip. It is a weapon for striking, but a skilled
  user can also entangle an opponent with it. It was not a common weapon; it
  was originally a farm tool used in herding and the like. In modern times it
  was used on slaves.


Polearms

 Spear
  The oldest kind of spear. Overall length 0.8-3 m, weight 1.2-3.5 kg. By
  length, spears are classified as short spears and long spears, and their
  uses differ. Its feature is a stone or metal spearhead on a wooden haft, and
  it is a weapon for thrusting. A short spear is a spear under 2 m, used for
  hunting and fighting since the Stone Age. It is one-handed, and besides
  thrusting it could also be thrown over short distances. In ancient Hittite
  times, a group tactic using soldiers with shields and short spears was born.
  A long spear, on the other hand, is a spear over 2 m, a weapon born after
  the short spear tactics were established. As spears grew longer in order to
  land the first blow in close combat, it was born in ancient Sumer. It was
  used in both hands, held at the hip. In the Middle Ages the use of the spear
  declined because more effective weapons appeared, but it became the basis of
  the polearms and strongly influenced them.

 Trident (also: fuscina, fucinula) (not in TOband)
  A "three-pronged spear". Overall length 1.5-1.8 m, weight 2-2.5 kg. A weapon
  said to have branched off from a fishing tool at the same time as the spear,
  used by Roman gladiators, galley sailors, peasant soldiers after Roman
  times, and others. Its feature is three spear-shaped points side by side on
  the end of the haft. The usual way to use it was to hold the trident in the
  right hand and a net in the left, catch the opponent with the net so he
  could not move, and then kill him with the trident.

 Lance (also: coronel)
  Overall length 2.5-4.2 m, weight 3.5-10 kg. A charging spear used by cavalry
  from the 16th to the end of the 19th century. It was also used in the joust
  of tournaments from the 13th to the 17th century. Lances are broadly of
  three types: for real combat, for jousting, and the type used in Eastern
  Europe. Their features differ with each type, but in general they are a
  sharp triangular cone in shape, with a widely flared hand guard, a thin
  grip, and behind it a thickened part for balance. All types were used held
  fixed under one arm. The point was spear-shaped for real combat, and a crown
  shape with three claws for jousting. Since the only purpose of a jousting
  lance is to knock the opponent off his horse, it has such a point in order
  to deliver a stronger shock while not wounding the opponent. Some also had
  grooves in the shaft so that they would break easily. The lances of Eastern
  Europe, on the other hand, are more practical: like a pike, they have a very
  long shaft with a sharp spearhead. Incidentally, the lance used by knights
  from the 7th to the 13th century was no different from a long spear, and the
  long spear used when charging on horseback was called a lance.

 Pike (also: pique) (not in TOband)
  Overall length 5-7 m, spearhead about 25 cm, weight 3.5-5 kg. A long spear
  used against cavalry by infantry from the 15th to the end of the 17th
  century. It is a weapon for thrusting, but it had an enormous effect as a
  threat to knights. You set it with both feet and both hands so that the
  point is at the height of a knight, and aim it at the charging cavalry.
  Because it has a longer reach than the cavalryman's lance, the cavalry
  hesitated to charge, and if they did charge, the pikeman could use their
  momentum to deal a heavy blow. In the later period it was used by infantry
  units that protected the musket-armed units while they loaded their bullets.

 Awl-Pike (not in TOband)
  An "awl spear". Overall length 2.5-3 m, spearhead 80-100 cm, weight 2.5-3
  kg. A long spear used against cavalry by infantry from the 13th to the 16th
  century. Its features are an extremely long spearhead, thin and sharp like
  an awl, and a round guard at the base of the spearhead. The spearhead is
  long and sharp because the knights of the 13th century covered their whole
  bodies in a coat of chain mail called a hauberk, and the weapon was made to
  stab a knight through the gaps in the chain. The round guard is to keep the
  spearhead from going in more deeply than necessary, and to receive the
  opponent's attacks. However, it is not a very practical weapon, and it is
  meaningless against enemies who are not wearing a hauberk.

 Lucerne Hammer (not in TOband)
  Overall length 3 m, head 40-50 cm, weight 3.5 kg. A kind of war hammer used
  by infantry from the 15th to the middle of the 16th century. Its features
  are a sharp hook on one side of the head, four projections on the opposite
  side, and a spearhead as sharp as a spear. With this head the lucerne hammer
  has four functions: thrusting, hooking, piercing the opponent's helmet with
  the hook, and snagging the opponent's clothing with the projections. For
  this reason the lucerne hammer can be said to be closer to the halberd than
  to a kind of war hammer. It can also be called a weapon that developed from
  the war hammer and came to resemble the halberd closely. The name Lucerne
  comes from the fact that the weapon was born in the canton of Lucerne
  (Luzern) in central Switzerland.

 Halberd
  The so-called "axe-spear" or "polearm". Overall length 2-3.5 m, head 30-50
  cm, weight 2.5-3.5 kg. Used by infantry from the 13th to the end of the 16th
  century. Its features lie in the complicated shape of its head. On one side
  there is an axe-like blade, on the opposite side a hook for piercing, and at
  the end of the head a spear-like point for thrusting. With this head it has
  four functions: hacking, thrusting, hooking, and piercing with the hook. It
  is more powerful than the spear. Because of its versatility, it became the
  main weapon of infantry: the spear part pierces cavalry, the hook breaks the
  opponent's helmet, and the axe part attacks, drags a cavalryman off his
  horse, or sweeps the legs of infantry. From the end of the 16th century to
  the 19th century, decorated ones for ceremonies were used by guardsmen at
  parades. Most illustrations are based on halberds of this period.

 Glaive (also: fauchard, cause, kuse)
  Overall length 2-3.5 m, blade length 60-70 cm, weight 2-3.5 kg. From the
  13th to the end of the 17th century it was used mainly as a weapon for
  guardsmen and for ceremonies. Its feature is a blade occupying the whole of
  the spearhead, with a shape like the falchion. That is, it is single-edged,
  the blade bulges into a crescent-shaped arc, and the back is straight. The
  tip is also pointed and can thrust, but swinging it to hack is more
  effective and is the usual way to use this weapon. It was not used much in
  real combat, and from the 16th century it became a completely ceremonial
  weapon. The spearheads of ceremonial glaives were carved with various
  patterns and emblems. A glaive with a hook on the side opposite the blade
  was rather for real combat, and was called a fauchard.

 Lochaber Axe (not in TOband)
  Overall length 2-2.5 m, axe blade length 40-50 cm, weight 2.2-2.5 kg. A war
  axe used by soldiers of Scottish peasant origin from the 16th to the 18th
  century. Its features are a very thin single-edged axe blade and a hook at
  the end. The hook was used to drag a mounted opponent down. The axe blade is
  for cutting and has no point for thrusting. The name Lochaber is the name of
  a region of Scotland, and it was given because the weapon was born there.
  The Jedburgh axe, a very similar weapon, likewise bears the name of a
  Scottish town. The Lochaber axe is a weapon of the United Kingdom, and there
  is no record at all of its use outside the United Kingdom.

 Scythe
  The so-called "great sickle". Overall length 2-2.5 m, blade length 70-90 cm,
  weight 2.2-2.5 kg. A weapon derived from the farm tool of the same name,
  used by peasant soldiers from the late 16th century to the 17th century. Its
  feature is a curved single-edged blade. Because tenant farmers who knew
  nothing about fighting used it in rebellions, the usual way to use it was to
  hold it at the hip and simply swing it around. In close combat, hacking with
  the blade and knocking the opponent down with the whole weapon were also
  widely used. Incidentally, the scythe that the Grim Reaper is said to carry
  has an angle of 90 degrees between the shaft and the blade, but the scythe
  actually used had a curved blade in the line of the shaft.


Missile Weapons

 Short Bow
  A "short bow". Overall length under 1 m, weight 0.5-0.8 kg. Effective range
  90 m, maximum range 225 m. A missile weapon that came into use for hunting
  at the end of the Paleolithic age. It was used by the armies of ancient
  Egypt, ancient Sumer, and others. Its feature is the shortness of the bow
  itself. Because its length was convenient to handle, it was also used on
  horseback. It was a weapon that required great skill to hit a target with,
  and a great deal of practice was needed.

 Long Bow
  A "long bow". Overall length 150-200 cm, weight 0.6-1 kg. Effective range
  150 m, maximum range 255 m. A missile weapon used by English archers from
  the 12th to the 15th century. The difference from the short bow is its
  length, and it had a very great power of penetration, enough to pierce plate
  mail. Its range was longer than that of the short bow, and the range could
  be lengthened further by shooting in an arc. However, to hit a target with
  this way of shooting, a far longer period of practice than usual was needed.

 Heavy Crossbow (called "Crossbow" in TOband)
  Overall length (stock) 60-100 cm, width 50-70 cm, weight 3-10 kg. Effective
  range 40-80 m, maximum range 100-425 m. A missile weapon used from the 10th
  to the 15th century; it is what is called a bowgun. Compared with the short
  bow and long bow, which require training, it spread widely in Europe because
  of its high performance: just by pulling the trigger it could shoot a more
  powerful bolt (quarrel) farther. It was so powerful that its use against
  fellow Christians was once banned, and its greatest drawback was that it
  took a long time to draw the steel string. Various methods were tried to
  solve this, but a revolutionary one was never found. This proved fatal, and
  it ended its life as a weapon in the middle of the 15th century. In the
  West, the crossbow was first used in ancient Greece in the 4th century BC.

 Light Crossbow (called "Bowgun" in TOband)
  This is the crossbow used for hunting and sport from the 16th century on. It
  became smaller, lighter, and easier to handle. Versions that fire stones or
  bullets instead of quarrels were also developed. It is still called a bowgun
  today and is used for sport and hunting.

 Sling (not in TOband)
  A "stone-throwing cord". Overall length 1 m, weight under 0.3 kg. Effective
  range 100 m, maximum range 120-180 m. A throwing weapon used for hunting
  since the Paleolithic age. It was also used by armies from ancient Israel,
  ancient Egypt, and ancient Greece to the Roman Empire. Its shape resembles
  an eyepatch: there is a piece of leather or cloth in the middle of a cord to
  wrap the shot, and another cord beyond that. To use it, you wind one cord
  around your hand, put a stone in the stone pouch, grip the other cord in the
  same hand, and swing the sling around over your head. When the stone has
  gained enough speed, you let go. The momentum of the swing then sends the
  stone flying. Merely throwing a stone is easy, but hitting a target takes
  great skill. It was a primitive weapon, but because of the relation between
  cost and power, armies used it until the time of the Crusades. In
  undeveloped regions it was used until the 19th century and frightened
  explorers.


Fist Weapons

 Cesti
  The so-called knuckle duster. A weapon worn on the fist, made of metal,
  intended to raise the striking power of a punch. There are various forms and
  they cannot be described in general, but the most common is the one now used
  as knuckles. There are also glove types and knuckle types with projections
  on the surface. They were used in ancient Greek boxing and by ancient Roman
  pugilists. There is no example of their use as regular military equipment.
